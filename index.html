What is a fantasy draft, actually?

01 — The idea

Instead of watching one team, you build your own team of real players from any club in the league. When those players score goals, keep clean sheets, or rack up assists in real matches, you earn points.

A "draft" just means how you get your players. Each player can only belong to one person in your league — once a friend picks a striker, that striker is off the board for everyone else.

Most leagues run a full season, and you'll manage your squad weekly: swapping injured players, setting your starting eleven, and choosing a captain.

Before you join a league, know:

  • How many teams are in your league (decides how many picks you get)
  • Whether it's a snake draft or an auction draft
  • The scoring rules — they're not identical everywhere
  • How often you can swap players (weekly or season-limited)

You build a lineup of real NFL players — a quarterback, running backs, receivers, and more — from any team. When they score touchdowns, pile up yards, or catch passes on Sunday, you earn points.

In the draft, each player is claimed by only one team in your league. Once a quarterback is picked, no one else can draft him. This is why knowing players beyond your favorite team pays off.

You set a new starting lineup every single week based on matchups, injuries, and byes — this is where fantasy football becomes a weekly habit, not a one-time event.

Before you join a league, know:

  • Whether it's standard, PPR, or half-PPR scoring (this changes who's valuable)
  • League size — 10 or 12 teams is most common
  • Your draft position — it changes your whole strategy
  • Whether trades and waivers are allowed mid-season

You assemble a roster of real NBA players. On most nights, several of your players are in action — every point, rebound, assist, steal, and block they get adds to your score.

During the draft, each player goes to one fantasy team only. With an 82-game season and players resting or getting hurt, depth matters as much as star power.

Unlike football's once-a-week rhythm, basketball moves fast — you'll be checking your lineup several times a week as game schedules shift.

Before you join a league, know:

  • Whether it's a points league or a categories league — they play very differently
  • How many games each team plays that week (schedule matters a lot)
  • Roster lock rules — can you swap players daily?
  • Injury and rest-day habits of the stars you're drafting

How draft day works

02 — Draft day

The most common format across every sport here is a snake draft. Picks go in order round one, then reverse for round two, then flip back again — like a snake winding down a board. Whoever picks last in round one picks first in round two, which keeps things fair.

Rookie manager tips

05 — Before you pick
Tip 01

Don't draft your favorite team. Spread picks across clubs so one bad match day doesn't wreck your score.

Tip 02

Midfielders who take penalties or free kicks are some of the safest picks — goals and assists both.

Tip 03

Check the fixture list before you draft. Easy upcoming matches make a player worth more short-term.

Tip 04

Save your captain pick for someone with a favorable matchup that week — it's worth double points.

Tip 05

Injuries happen. Keep one or two bench players who could start if a starter goes down.

Tip 06

Early picks matter most, but mid-to-late rounds are where leagues are actually won or lost.

Tip 01

Running backs tend to get drafted early because there are fewer reliable ones than at other positions.

Tip 02

Check the bye week schedule before drafting two players at the same position for the same week.

Tip 03

A "handcuff" is the backup to your star running back — worth rostering in case of injury.

Tip 04

Don't draft a kicker or defense early. They're replaceable almost every week off the waiver wire.

Tip 05

Know your league's scoring before you draft — PPR makes pass-catching running backs far more valuable.

Tip 06

Target receivers whose quarterback throws often. The offense's scheme matters as much as talent.

Tip 01

In points leagues, prioritize players who do a little of everything — points, boards, and assists all add up.

Tip 02

In category leagues, consider "punting" a category you can't win and loading up on the rest.

Tip 03

Check which teams play more games that week — more games usually means more fantasy points.

Tip 04

Injury-prone stars can still be worth drafting, just not with your very first pick.

Tip 05

Streaming — picking up a player just for one favorable game — is a legit weekly strategy.

Tip 06

Centers and point guards tend to be the scarcest positions — don't wait too long on either.

Quick glossary

06 — Terms to know

The pool of undrafted players. If someone breaks out mid-season, you can claim them, usually dropping someone else to make room.

The 11 players from your squad you set to score points that week. Everyone else sits on your bench and earns nothing.

Your captain earns double points that week. The vice-captain is the backup if your captain doesn't play.

Swapping one of your players for a different one. Extra transfers beyond your free limit usually cost points.

An alternative to a snake draft — everyone gets a fake budget and bids on players one at a time.

When a team finishes a match without conceding. Good news for owners of their goalkeeper or defenders.

Points Per Reception — a scoring style where every catch earns a point, making pass-catchers more valuable.

A lineup slot that can be filled by a running back, wide receiver, or tight end — whichever you choose.

Every NFL team sits out one week during the season. A player on bye scores zero, so plan your lineup around it.

Drafting the backup to a star running back, so you're covered if the starter gets hurt.

The pool of undrafted free agents. A breakout player mid-season is usually picked up here first.

Defense/Special Teams — you draft an entire team's defensive unit as one roster spot.

Every stat is converted into a single point total — simplest format, close to real basketball value.

You compete head-to-head across several separate stats, like rebounds or steals, rather than one combined score.

Deliberately giving up on one stat category to stack your roster with strength in the rest.

Picking up a bench player just because they have a favorable game or two that week, then dropping them after.

Reaching double digits in two stat categories in one game — commonly points and rebounds, or points and assists.

Two games on consecutive nights. Stars sometimes rest for one, which matters for your weekly lineup.
